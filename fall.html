Pegasus

Of course, this constellation is included to honor our university's symbol. Look southwest after 9 p.m. to find 4 bright stars that make a square. The fainter stars that extend outward in front of the square make up Pegasus's head and front legs. In Greek Mythology, Pegasus appeared from Medusa after Perseus beheaded her. Pegasus is also linked to the Hippocrene Spring on Mt. Helican in Greece that is a source of creativity and inspiration for the arts.

×

Andromeda

The constellation named for the woman saved by Perseus can be located in the night sky by first finding the square of the stars in Pegasus, then follow the chain of stars from the top left towards northwest. Andromeda was the daughter of Cassiopeia and was chained to a rock as a human sacrifice to Poseidon, god of the sea. Perseus flew in on the back of Pegasus and freed her.

×

Cassiopeia

Known as the vain queen, this large constellation is easy to spot in the northern sky. Look for a large "W." Fun fact, this constellation contains a supernova, SN1572. According to Greek Mythology, Queen Cassiopeia declared she was more beautiful than the Nereids (sea nymphs). The god, Poseidon became angry and sent his sea monster to destroy their empire. Cassiopeia had to send her daughter, Andromeda as a sacrifice to stop the sea monster's destruction of their kingdom.
